About

The interesting part of hardware isn't the schematic — it's everything that happens after, when a design has to survive being manufactured, soldered, and switched on.

I'm an Electrical & Electronic Engineering graduate with hands-on time across ESP32 and RP2040 platforms, multi-layer PCB layout, and firmware debugging in C. During an internship at Sri Lanka Telecom I carried an early-stage prototype through to a field-deployed, mass-production-ready device — the kind of process that teaches you how much of hardware engineering is triage, not design.

My final year project, JumperZ Studio, lives at that same intersection: a programmable breadboard built around a custom RP2040/ESP32 mainboard, driving a switching matrix across 400 addressable LEDs, controlled entirely from a desktop application I designed and built from scratch in Python.

Outside the FYP I've laid out complete boards in EasyEDA and led the mechanical and sensor design across two competition Micromouse robots, built with a 5-person team over two years. Firmware is where I'm investing time now — the hardware side is solid; getting the chips to do exactly what I intend is the next chapter.

Final Year Project

JumperZ Studio

EDA · Embedded · Desktop

A programmable breadboard: draw a connection on screen and it physically routes current through real hardware, instantly.

Built around a custom RP2040/ESP32 mainboard driving a CH446Q-based analog switching matrix across 400 individually addressable WS2812B LEDs, with live current and voltage sensing over USB. The desktop application — designed and built solo in Python with PySide6 — uses a strict layered architecture, translating visual connections into firmware commands in real time.
